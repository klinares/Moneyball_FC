\begin{abstract}
This study investigates the determinants of player market value in elite
European football using a cross-classified multilevel model applied to
7,023 observations between 2013 and 2024. By disentangling individual
reputation from club context, we identify distinct non-linear trends in
market evolution and biological aging. Our results reveal a ``Boom and
Bust'' market cycle, a strict ``Inverted-U'' career arc peaking at age
27, and a significant 42\% valuation premium for key starters (``Squad
Status''). Furthermore, we find evidence of a ``Diversity Premium,''
where clubs with higher international representation garner higher
player valuations, contrasting with a ``Domestic Discount'' for local
players.
\end{abstract}

\noindent%
{\it Keywords:} football economics, multilevel modeling, player
valuation, club prestige, UEFA coefficient, sports analytics

\section{Introduction and Research
Motivation}\label{introduction-and-research-motivation}

The valuation of elite athletes in the European soccer transfer market
has evolved from a subjective scouting exercise into a global financial
industry exceeding €7 billion annually. In this high-stakes environment,
accurate valuation is critical for club solvency and competitive
balance. In this study, we operationalize market value using
crowd-sourced estimates from Transfermarkt. This metric has been widely
validated in sports economics literature as a robust proxy for actual
transfer fees and serves as a leading indicator of player market
valuation based on demand.

Foundational research in sports economics has long sought to isolate the
determinants of this value. Frick (2007) established that while hard
performance metrics like goals and assists are primary drivers,
valuation follows a strict inverted-U biological trajectory, typically
peaking between ages 27 and 28 before depreciating rapidly. Beyond
on-pitch performance, Franck and Nüesch (2012) empirically demonstrated
the superstar effect. They found that non-performance factors such as
media popularity and brand visibility can magnify a player's value
significantly, especially at the top end of the wage distribution.
However, these existing studies often model players as static units
nested within a single club. This approach fails to account for the
dynamic reality of modern football, where players transfer between teams
and accumulate value from both their portable human capital (such as
experience and age) and the institutional prestige of their current
employer.

Neglecting this cross-classified structure, where player and club
effects intersect rather than nest, likely misestimates the true drivers
of market value. We address this methodological limitation by applying a
cross-classified multilevel model to a decade of transfer data (2013 to
2024). By disentangling individual reputation from club context, we aim
to answer four critical questions that remain under-explored in nested
models:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Market Evolution (RQ1):} How has the transfer market evolved
  annually compared to the 2013 baseline? We believe that understanding
  the nonlinear trajectory of market inflation is essential for
  distinguishing real player value growth from sector-wide inflation.
\item
  \textbf{Biological Career Arc (RQ2):} How does market value change as
  a player moves through their career? By modeling the nonlinear effect
  of age, we aim to validate Frick's (2007) peak age hypothesis and
  identify the precise window where physical peak and experience
  intersect.
\item
  \textbf{Squad Status (RQ3):} Does playing more minutes than the
  average teammate serve as a primary driver of valuation? While goals
  capture output, they bias towards attackers. We propose that squad
  status (measured by relative minutes played) is a more universal
  signal of manager trust and player utility across all positions.
\item
  \textbf{Contextual Premiums (RQ4):} Is there a premium for club
  diversity and a discount for domestic players? As the labor market
  globalizes, we investigate whether clubs with broader international
  recruitment generate higher valuations and whether domestic players
  face a valuation discount compared to imported talent.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method}\label{method}

\emph{Data Source.} We obtained data from
\href{https://www.transfermarkt.com/}{Transfermarkt} and
\href{https://www.uefa.com/}{Union of European Football Association}
(UEFA) official rankings, which are both publicly available websites. At
the time of this study, Transfermarkt does not have free API access to
their data repository. Instead, the R package
\href{https://jaseziv.github.io/worldfootballR/articles/load-scraped-data.html}{worldfootballR}
allows us to scrape data from their website. However, there are several
limitations to this package such as timeout requests and failure to
capture dynamic JavaScript tables on webpages. Fortunately, we found the
necessary data through Kaggle
\href{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/davidcariboo/player-scores?resource=download}{\textbf{Football
Data from Transfermarkt}}, which allowed us to focus on the modeling
aspect of this project following data restructuring and predictor
variable engineering.

\emph{Sample Characteristics.} We used the 10-year averaged UEFA
coefficient rankings\footnote{UEFA coefficient rankings are a
  points-based system used by European football's governing body to rank
  national associations and clubs based on their performance in European
  competitions, which determines qualification spots and seeding for
  tournaments like the Champions League and Europa League.} to select
the top 21 European soccer teams as our first stage sampling. In our
second stage nonrandom sampling, we selected soccer players that played
for a full season in one of the 21 clubs from 2013 to 2024. This
resulted in 2,215 male players representing 7,168 seasons. However,
after examining missing data (which mostly came from missing information
on citizenship status), we were left with 2,159 players representing
7,023 seasons. This represents a 2.5\% loss of player data and a 2\%
loss in season data. The majority of players across clubs and years were
in their mid-20s (\emph{M} = 26.5, \emph{SD} = 4.6). In this sample,
players on average played 3.2 (\emph{SD} = 2.9) seasons. If we were to
drop players that had less than two seasons to examine player
trajectories, we would lose 62\% of our sample. Figure 1 shows the
number of players in each of the 21 clubs across the years.

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{MLM_Determinants_of_player_market_value_draft_files/figure-pdf/unnamed-chunk-3-1.pdf}}

\emph{Variable Selection and Normalization.} Our outcome of interest is
a player's yearly market valuation, which we convert from euros to US
dollars. Market valuation in euros varies by month, so we first
calculated the yearly average for each player prior to transforming to
USD. We log-transform market value to 2024 USD because financial data
follows a Pareto distribution rather than a normal distribution. Keeping
the outcome on the raw scale would result in extremely large residuals
for high-value players and small residuals for low-value players, which
violates the assumption of homoscedasticity. Log-transforming the
outcome compresses the extreme outliers by pulling the long right tail
to make the distribution more normal. Therefore, this adjustment allows
us to compare the real economic power of a player in 2013 versus 2024
without the noise of currency fluctuation.\footnote{We handle exchange
  rate fluctuation by using historical exchange rates to convert our
  values to USD. We also adjust market value to 2024 USD because 10
  million dollars in 2013 is not the same as 10 million in 2024 due to
  inflation. By keeping the raw values, we would lose information about
  real economic value.} Figure 2 shows that market value has substantial
variability across years, yet most values are at the lower end, which is
common for salary data. When we log-transform our outcome, we bring the
lower values up and the higher values down.

To ensure meaningful model intercepts and convergence, we applied the
following centering strategies:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Outcome:} We used the natural log of inflation-adjusted market
  value (\texttt{usd\_revenue\_2024\_log}).
\item
  \textbf{Time:} We centered season year at the minimum value, making
  2013 equal to 0 (\texttt{season\_year\_c}).
\item
  \textbf{Player Traits:} We applied grand-mean centering to age
  (\texttt{age\_c}) and height (\texttt{height\_c}).
\item
  \textbf{Squad Status:} We standardized minutes played (Z-score)
  relative to each specific club-year. This isolates a player's status
  as a starter or bench player relative to their current teammates and
  removes the noise of team-level rotation policies.
\item
  \textbf{Club Context:} We grand-mean centered the proportion of
  foreign players (\texttt{foreign\_players}).
\item
  \textbf{Categorical Controls:} We included region, position, and
  citizenship status (coded as 1 for citizens of the club country).
\end{itemize}

\emph{Data Assumptions:} We assume that variables such as height and
positions are time invariant for the duration of this study. At the club
level, we assume mutually exclusiveness in that players can only belong
to one club in a given year. Although a player could have switched clubs
mid-season, we keep this constant for model simplicity. We also assume
that the relationship between age and log-market value is non-linear and
parabolic so we introduce a quadratic term on age. Because we excluded
observations with missing data (listwise deletion), we assume the data
are Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) or that the proportion of
missing cases (\textasciitilde2\%) is negligible and does not introduce
systematic bias.

\section{Modeling Approach}\label{modeling-approach}

We employ a cross-classified multilevel linear model to partition
variance across observations (Level 1), players (Level 2a), and clubs
(Level 2b). This structure explicitily accounts for players transferring
between clubs and thus treating player and club as cross random factors
rather than a strict three level hierarchy. We found that in this
sample, almost 20\% of players had been in two to seven (17\% in two
clubs) clubs which was the basis for fitting a cross-classified model to
these data. Figure 3 is a visual of a cross-classified model example of
three players that either move through clubs or remain in the same club
throughout their career. Players can change teams over their span of
their careers meaning that the player context can change. They may be a
star player in one team and a bench player on a different team that they
switch to. In a cross-classified model, the player is the constant unit
(i), but the club (j) changes over time (t). If player (I) plays for
club (j1) in year (t0) but switches to club (j2) in year (t1) the fixed
effect in the model, such as minutes played, updates the players status,
as opposed to having static or average predictors. Club attributes, such
as foreign players and UEFA scores, ensures that when player move clubs,
the model swaps the values of \(X_j1\), for \(X_j2\). Player's
characteristics, for instance age and height, move with the player
across time.

\subsection{Model Specification}\label{model-specification}

To formally describe our analysis, we utilize a hierarchical notation
system that decomposes the model into observation, player, and club
levels. This analysis clarifies how the cross-classified structure
(\(i\) players crossed with \(j\) clubs) contributes to the variance in
market value.

Level 1: Observation Model (Time-Varying Effects)

At the observation level (time \(t\), player \(i\), club \(j\)), the
log-transformed market value is a function of a player-club specific
intercept (\(\pi_{0ij}\)), temporal trends, biological aging, and
dynamic performance metrics:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log(\text{Revenue})_{tij} &= \pi_{0ij} \\
&+ \pi_{1j}(\text{Year}_{t}) + \pi_{2}(\text{Year}^2_{t}) + \pi_{3}(\text{Year}^3_{t}) \\
&+ \pi_{4}(\text{Age}_{it}) + \pi_{5}(\text{Age}_{it})^2 \\
&+ \pi_{6}(\text{Mins}_{Z,it}) + \pi_{7}(\text{Goals}^*_{it}) + \pi_{8}(\text{Assists}^*_{it}) \\
&+ \pi_{9}(\text{Red}_{it}) + \pi_{10}(\text{Yellow}_{it}) \\
&+ \epsilon_{tij}
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(\epsilon_{tij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{\epsilon})\).

Level 2a: Player Model (Time-Invariant Traits)

The player-club intercept (\(\pi_{0ij}\)) is determined by static
physical and demographic characteristics, citizenship status relative to
the club, and a player-specific random effect:

\[
\pi_{0ij} = \beta_{0j} + \gamma_{1}(\text{Height}_{i}) + \gamma_{2}(\text{Citizen}_{ij}) + \sum_{p} \gamma_{p} \mathbb{1}_{\text{Position}_i=p} + \sum_{r} \gamma_{r} \mathbb{1}_{\text{Region}_i=r} + u_{0i}
\]

where \(u_{0i} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{u})\).

Level 2b: Club Model (Contextual Effects)

The club-level baseline (\(\beta_{0j}\)) and the linear time slope
(\(\pi_{1j}\)) are modeled as functions of club internationalization and
club-specific random effects:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\beta_{0j} &= \delta_{00} + \delta_{01}(\text{Foreign}_{tj}) + v_{0j} \\
\pi_{1j} &= \delta_{10} + v_{1j} \\
\pi_{2} &= \delta_{20} \quad \text{(fixed across clubs)} \\
\pi_{3} &= \delta_{30} \quad \text{(fixed across clubs)}
\end{aligned}
\]

where
\(\begin{pmatrix} v_{0j} \\ v_{1j} \end{pmatrix} \sim N \left( \mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{club} \right)\),
with
\(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{club} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2_{v_0} & \sigma_{v_0v_1} \\ \sigma_{v_0v_1} & \sigma^2_{v_1} \end{pmatrix}\).

Interpretation of Cross-Classification:

Player effects (\(u_{0i}\)): Capture persistent individual heterogeneity
(e.g., reputation, marketability) that follows a player across clubs.

Club effects (\(v_{0j}, v_{1j}\)): Capture the baseline prestige and
temporal trajectory of the club itself, which affects all players on the
roster.

Cross-classified structure: A player's market value at time \(t\)
depends on both their intrinsic ``brand'' (\(u_{0i}\)) and the club they
are currently with (\(v_{0j} + v_{1j} \cdot \text{Year}_t\)).

\section{Results}\label{results}

\textbf{Model Building and Comparison}

We fitted a series of nested multilevel models to identify the optimal
specification. We started with a null model containing only random
intercepts for players and clubs to establish baseline variance
partitioning. We then systematically added temporal trends (linear,
quadratic, and cubic), random slopes for club trajectories, player-level
predictors, and finally club-level contextual factors. We compared
models using likelihood ratio tests (LRT) with REML estimation and
assessed fit using AIC and BIC criteria.

\textbf{Market Evolution (RQ1)}

Our model reveals a significant cubic trend (p \textless{} .001) in
market evolution. As shown in Figure 6A, the market followed an S-shaped
trajectory. We observed stagnation from 2013 to 2014, followed by a
rapid inflationary boom from 2015 to 2019, and then a post-2020 plateau.
This confirms that football market inflation is cyclical rather than
constant.

\textbf{Biological Career Arc (RQ2)}

We observe a strong quadratic effect for age (p \textless{} .001).
Figure 6B illustrates the inverted-U shape, where value peaks during a
player's prime (approximately age 27) and declines significantly in the
veteran stage.

\textbf{Squad Status Premium (RQ3)}

Our results show that squad status is a massive driver of valuation.
Because we standardized minutes played, the coefficient (approximately
0.35) indicates that a one standard deviation increase in playing time
is associated with a 42\% increase in market value. This confirms that
being a starter is financially distinct from being a rotation player.

\textbf{Contextual Effects (RQ4)}

We find evidence of a diversity premium. A 10 percentage point increase
in a club's foreign player proportion increases average player value by
2\%. Conversely, we find a domestic discount where homegrown citizens
are valued 17\% lower than foreign imports.

\section{Club-Level Heterogeneity}\label{club-level-heterogeneity}

Figure 7 displays the estimated club random effects from our
cross-classified model. We find substantial heterogeneity in both
baseline valuations and temporal trajectories.

\subsection{Baseline Premiums and
Discounts}\label{baseline-premiums-and-discounts}

Manchester United exhibits the largest effect. When controlling for age,
position, and performance, United players are valued 68\% higher than
league average. The 95\% confidence interval ranges from 31\% to 76\%.
Barcelona shows a 56\% premium (95\% CI from 29\% to 93\%), while Paris
Saint-Germain shows a 26\% premium (95\% CI from 6\% to 53\%). Chelsea,
Manchester City, and Bayern Munich also show significant premiums of
approximately 22\% to 25\%.

Conversely, seven clubs show significant discounts. Sevilla faces the
steepest penalty, with players valued 28\% below league average (95\% CI
from -42\% to -23\%). Porto shows a 27\% discount, Benfica shows 22\%,
Roma shows 21\%, Napoli shows 17\%, Villarreal shows 16\%, and Tottenham
shows 14\%.

\subsection{Diverging Temporal
Trajectories}\label{diverging-temporal-trajectories}

Our random slopes reveal that club fortunes diverged over the study
period. Three clubs showed significantly faster appreciation than league
average. Arsenal showed growth of 2.9\% per year (95\% CI from 0.8\% to
5.0\%), Liverpool showed 2.7\% per year (95\% CI from 0.7\% to 4.8\%),
and Tottenham showed 2.8\% per year (95\% CI from 0.8\% to 4.8\%). These
trajectories coincide with improved sporting performance and, for
Liverpool, a Champions League victory in 2019.

Four clubs experienced significantly slower growth or faster decline.
Manchester United declined 2.8\% per year (95\% CI from -4.5\% to
-1.0\%), Borussia Dortmund declined 2.3\% per year (95\% CI from -4.1\%
to -0.4\%), Juventus declined 2.3\% per year (95\% CI from -4.1\% to
-0.5\%), and Barcelona declined 1.8\% per year (95\% CI from -3.5\% to
-0.1\%). Despite high baseline premiums, these clubs saw their market
advantages erode over time.

\section{Findings}\label{findings}

Our study demonstrates that player market value is driven primarily by
individual reputation and squad status, but is significantly moderated
by club context and market cycles. Our cross-classified approach reveals
that 78\% of variance can be attributed to persistent player effects
while accounting for dynamic club contexts.

While our cross-classified model demonstrates strong predictive power
(R-squared approximately 0.89), several limitations warrant
consideration. First, although the likelihood ratio test supported
including random slopes for club trajectories, we should interpret the
variance estimates with caution given the relatively small number of
unique clubs (N = 21). Second, our diagnostic plots indicated deviations
from normality in the extreme tails of the residual distribution. While
our large sample size (N = 7,023) ensures robust estimation of fixed
effects, future iterations could employ robust standard errors or a
Gamma GLMM to better address these extremes. Finally, predictors such as
minutes played are likely endogenous, since better players naturally
accrue more playing time. We should interpret our estimates as
associations reflecting squad status rather than purely causal effects.

Ultimately, our research highlights the necessity of moving beyond
static, single-level analyses to capture the true complexity of the
football transfer market. By rigorously modeling the cross-classified
intersection of individual player trajectories and dynamic club
contexts, we provide a more accurate lens for valuing talent in a
hyper-inflated economy. As the industry continues to professionalize,
such multidimensional frameworks (which account for the interplay of
human capital, institutional prestige, and market cycles) will remain
indispensable for clubs seeking to distinguish genuine value from market
noise.
