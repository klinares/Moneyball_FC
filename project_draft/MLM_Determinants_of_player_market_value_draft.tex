\begin{abstract}
This study investigates the determinants of player market values in
elite European football using a three-level cross-classified
mixed-effects model. We analyzed 7,023 season-year observations of 2,215
players across 21 elite clubs from 2013 to 2024. The primary research
question examines how individual performance metrics, player
characteristics, and club-level prestige jointly predict market
valuations. Results demonstrate that club prestige, measured by the UEFA
10-year coefficient, explains 47.9\% of between-club variance in player
values (p\textless0.001), independent of individual performance.
Additionally, each goal scored increases market value by 3.75\%
(p\textless0.001), while each assist adds 4.95\% (p\textless0.001).
Positional effects reveal that attackers command approximately 71.1\%
premiums over goalkeepers (p\textless0.001). The full model achieves a
conditional R2 of 0.793. These findings provide evidence for a
substantial ``halo effect,'' whereby players at elite clubs command
valuation premiums independent of individual performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

The global football transfer market has grown exponentially, with annual
expenditures exceeding €7 billion. Consequently, understanding the
determinants of player market values is critical for clubs seeking
competitive advantages. While traditional scouting emphasized subjective
assessments, the ``Moneyball'' revolution demonstrated that data-driven
approaches could identify undervalued players by focusing on objective
metrics (Herm et al., 2014). Crowd-sourced platforms like Transfermarkt
provide real-time valuations that correlate strongly (r\textgreater0.90)
with actual transfer fees (Herm et al., 2014).

However, player market values likely reflect contextual factors such as
club reputation and competitive success, in addition to individual
performance. Previous research suggests that 15-20\% of salary variation
is attributable to team-level factors (Franck \& Nüesch, 2012). This
raises a critical question: to what extent do club-level characteristics
create a ``halo effect'' on player valuations? This study addresses
three objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Quantify individual performance effects:} Determine the
  marginal impact of measurable metrics (goals, assists) on market
  values.
\item
  \textbf{Assess club prestige effects:} Estimate the influence of
  club-level prestige (UEFA coefficients) on valuations beyond
  individual characteristics.
\item
  \textbf{Model multilevel structure:} Account for the cross-classified
  nature of football data, where repeated measurements are nested within
  players, and players transfer between clubs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\textbf{Theoretical
Framework}}{Theoretical Framework}}\label{theoretical-framework}

We integrate human capital theory with signaling theory. Human capital
theory posits that market value reflects an individual's productivity
potential (goals, assists). Signaling theory suggests that club
affiliation serves as a quality signal; playing for elite clubs
indicates higher quality because these clubs maintain higher selection
standards.

Herm et al.~(2014) established the validity of crowd-sourced market
valuations, identifying performance, age, and club prestige as key
attributes. While performance metrics consistently emerge as significant
predictors, the relative importance of different dimensions remains
contested. Furthermore, prior research rarely employs multilevel models
capable of attributing variance to individual versus team-level factors.

Franck and Nüesch (2012) demonstrated that team-level factors like brand
value significantly influence compensation. However, most research
treats club affiliation as a fixed effect or omits it, potentially
biasing estimates. Additionally, few studies account for player
mobility, which violates strict nesting assumptions. To address this, we
utilize the UEFA coefficient as an objective measure of club prestige
based on historical performance. We propose the following hypotheses:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{H1:} Player performance metrics will exhibit significant
  positive associations with market value.
\item
  \textbf{H2:} Club-level UEFA coefficients will predict player market
  values beyond individual characteristics.
\item
  \textbf{H3:} Player age will demonstrate a negative relationship with
  market value.
\item
  \textbf{H4:} Attacking players will command significant premiums over
  defensive players.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\textbf{Data Source and Sample} Data were obtained from Transfermarkt
and UEFA official rankings. The sample consists of 7,023 season-year
observations representing 2,215 unique players across 21 elite European
football clubs from 2013 to 2024. Clubs were selected based on
consistent presence in top UEFA rankings.

\textbf{Variables}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Outcome:} \emph{Market Value} (2024 USD), log-transformed to
  address right skewness.
\item
  \textbf{Level 1 Predictors (Time-Varying):} \emph{Season Year}
  (2013-2024), \emph{Goals}, \emph{Assists}, and \emph{Yellow Cards}.
\item
  \textbf{Level 2 Predictors (Player-Invariant):} \emph{Position}
  (Goalkeeper, Defender, Midfield, Attack), \emph{Age} (grand-mean
  centered at 26.5), \emph{Height} (grand-mean centered at 182.4 cm),
  \emph{Birth Region}, and \emph{Citizenship of Club Country}.
\item
  \textbf{Level 3 Predictor (Club-Level):} \emph{UEFA Coefficient}
  (grand-mean centered at 194.36).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Data Structure} The data exhibit a cross-classified structure.
Measurements (Level 1) are nested within players (Level 2). However,
players are not strictly nested within clubs because 456 players
(20.6\%) transferred clubs during the observation window. We model
player and club as crossed random effects: (1∣player\_id)+(1∣club\_id).
